ITIL Specialty Certificate (e.g., BRM / High-Velocity IT)

LAST-MINUTE CRAM (SPECIALTY): Focus deeply on specialized practices like Business Relationship Management (BRM), High-Velocity IT ethics, and complex Cloud Provider integrations.

Set 2 (Questions 51-100)

Q51. What is the primary purpose of a 'Digital Product' in High-Velocity IT? (A) To sell hardware (B) To package IT services, information, and people into a distinct offering that directly creates value for or on behalf of a customer (C) To increase the IT budget (D) To write as much code as possible
Answer: B - Explanation: A digital product isn't just code; it's the entire package (hosting, support, functionality) designed to fulfill a specific customer need.
Q52. How does the 'Business Relationship Manager' (BRM) handle conflicting demands between different business units? (A) By ignoring the conflicts (B) By acting as a strategic mediator, aligning the competing demands against the overarching enterprise strategy to prioritize the IT portfolio (C) By giving priority to whoever shouts loudest (D) By building whatever is asked for, doubling the IT budget
Answer: B - Explanation: BRMs don't just say 'yes' to everything. They align requests with the enterprise's strategic goals to maximize overall value, which often means saying 'no' to lower-priority requests.
Q53. In Lean IT, what is 'Overproduction'? (A) Writing defect-free code (B) Building features or generating reports that the customer did not ask for and does not use (C) Working overtime (D) Buying too many servers
Answer: B - Explanation: Overproduction is a massive waste. Building a 'cool' feature that nobody uses consumes development time, testing time, and creates future technical debt for zero value.
Q54. What is the 'Theory of Constraints' (ToC)? (A) A theory about legal contracts (B) A methodology stating that any manageable system is limited in achieving more of its goals by a very small number of constraints; there is always at least one constraint (bottleneck) (C) A theory about physical gravity (D) A programming language
Answer: B - Explanation: Identifying the primary bottleneck in your value stream and elevating it is the fastest way to increase overall system throughput.
Q55. If testing is identified as the constraint in a software delivery value stream, what happens if you double the number of developers? (A) The software ships twice as fast (B) Work piles up faster in front of the testing team, increasing WIP (Work in Progress), reducing flow, and potentially slowing down the overall delivery (C) Testing gets faster naturally (D) The constraint disappears
Answer: B - Explanation: Improving any step *other* than the bottleneck is an illusion. It just creates more inventory (unfinished work) waiting at the bottleneck.
Q56. What does 'WIP Limits' (Work in Progress limits) achieve in a Kanban system? (A) They force people to take days off (B) They prevent teams from starting new work until existing work is finished, forcing the resolution of bottlenecks and improving overall flow time (C) They limit how much money can be spent (D) They limit the number of users on a system
Answer: B - Explanation: Limiting WIP is counter-intuitive but essential. 'Stop starting, start finishing'. High WIP hides bottlenecks and drastically increases lead time.
Q57. What is 'Lead Time'? (A) The time it takes a manager to make a decision (B) The total time elapsed from when a customer makes a request until that request is fulfilled and delivered back to the customer (C) The time a developer spends coding (D) The time it takes a server to boot
Answer: B - Explanation: Lead Time is the ultimate metric of speed from the customer's perspective. It includes all waiting, processing, and testing time.
Q58. What is 'Cycle Time'? (A) The time the customer waits (B) The time it takes for a team to complete a specific task once they actually start working on it (excluding the time it sat waiting in the backlog) (C) The time to reboot a router (D) An annual review cycle
Answer: B - Explanation: Cycle Time measures team efficiency. Lead Time measures system efficiency. High Lead Time but low Cycle Time means work is spending most of its life waiting in queues.
Q59. How does 'Value Stream Mapping' address Wait Time? (A) By firing employees (B) By visually exposing how much of the total Lead Time is spent actually adding value (processing time) versus waiting in queues, often revealing that 90% of time is waiting (C) By ignoring it (D) By buying faster computers
Answer: B - Explanation: VSM often shows that a 30-day process only has 4 hours of actual work. The key to speed is eliminating the 29.5 days of waiting, not making the 4 hours of work slightly faster.
Q60. What is a 'Minimum Viable Process'? (A) Having no process at all (B) Applying the MVP concept to process design, creating just enough governance to achieve the objective safely, and iterating to add complexity only when proven necessary (C) The cheapest way to do something (D) A process designed by interns
Answer: B - Explanation: Rather than designing a massive, bureaucratic 'perfect' process upfront, design the leanest safe process, test it in reality, and adapt.
Q61. In High-Velocity environments, what replaces the traditional, heavy Change Advisory Board (CAB)? (A) CEO approval for everything (B) Peer review, automated testing pipelines, and delegating change authority to the teams closest to the work (C) No approval whatsoever (D) External auditors
Answer: B - Explanation: A CAB meeting once a week cannot support 50 deployments a day. Trust is placed in automated guardrails (CI/CD) and local team expertise.
Q62. What is 'Continuous Integration' (CI)? (A) Integrating two companies after a merger (B) Developers merging their code changes into a central repository frequently (multiple times a day), followed by automated builds and tests (C) Continuously hiring staff (D) Manual testing
Answer: B - Explanation: CI prevents 'integration hell' at the end of a project. Small, frequent merges ensure conflicts are resolved immediately while the context is fresh.
Q63. What distinguishes 'Continuous Delivery' from 'Continuous Deployment'? (A) They form a waterfall (B) Continuous Delivery ensures the code is *always* in a deployable state, but a human pushes the button to release it. Continuous Deployment means the pipeline automatically pushes it to live users without human intervention (C) There is no difference (D) Delivery is hardware, deployment is software
Answer: B - Explanation: Continuous Deployment is the holy grail of speed, relying entirely on the extreme robust nature of the automated test suite to prevent catastrophic failure.
Q64. What is 'Immutable Infrastructure'? (A) Hardware that never breaks (B) An approach where servers are never modified after they are deployed. If an update is needed, a completely new server is built from a patched image to replace the old one (C) A locked down server room (D) Infrastructure that cannot be hacked
Answer: B - Explanation: This eliminates 'Configuration Drift' (where two supposedly identical servers drift apart over time due to manual tweaks). It treats servers like disposable cattle, not beloved pets.
Q65. How does 'Infrastructure as Code' (IaC) support Immutable Infrastructure? (A) By physically moving servers (B) By allowing defining the entire server configuration in version-controlled text files, so a new, identical replacement server can be automatically spun up in seconds (C) By writing manuals (D) By using spreadsheets
Answer: B - Explanation: IaC ensures identical environments. Dev, Staging, and Production are all built from the exact same code template, eliminating the 'It works on my machine' problem.
Q66. What is the core ethos behind the SRE concept of 'Toil Management'? (A) Making people work harder (B) Engineering toil out of the system. An SRE should ideally spend no more than 50% of their time on operational toil, using the rest to automate that toil away (C) Accepting that IT is hard work (D) Hiring more lower-level staff to do the toil
Answer: B - Explanation: If a service grows 10x, you shouldn't need to hire 10x more operators. You automate the operational work so it scales non-linearly.
Q67. What is a 'Service Level Indicator' (SLI) in SRE? (A) A contract (B) A carefully defined quantitative measure of some aspect of the level of service that is provided (e.g., the proportion of HTTP GET requests that succeed) (C) A managerial objective (D) The physical speed of a network
Answer: B - Explanation: The SLI is the actual metric being read from the system in real-time.
Q68. What is a 'Service Level Objective' (SLO) in SRE? (A) An SLI (B) A target value or range of values for a service level that is measured by an SLI (e.g., 99.9% of HTTP GET requests will succeed) (C) A punishment for failure (D) A customer's dream requirement
Answer: B - Explanation: The SLO is the internal target. If the SLI drops below the SLO, the team knows they have a problem.
Q69. How does an SLO differ from an SLA? (A) They are identical (B) An SLA is an external legal/business contract that often carries financial penalties for failure. An SLO is a tighter internal target used to manage the service proactively before the SLA breaches (C) SLAs are only for hardware (D) SLOs only measure cost
Answer: B - Explanation: If the external SLA is 99.5%, the internal engineering SLO might be 99.9%. Breaching the SLO triggers alarms; breaching the SLA triggers lawsuits.
Q70. What is the fundamental mechanism of an 'Error Budget'? (A) 100% minus the SLO (e.g., if SLO is 99.9%, the budget is 0.1% downtime). It represents the acceptable level of failure allowed per month/quarter (B) A financial reserve (C) A limit on the number of developers (D) A bug tracking tool
Answer: A - Explanation: It embraces the fact that 100% reliability is impossible and too expensive. The 0.1% budget allows the team to take risks and deploy fast. Once the 0.1% is burned, deployments stop.
Q71. What is 'Blameless Post-Mortem' designed to counteract? (A) Over-spending (B) The natural human urge to find a scapegoat ('human error'), which drives mistakes underground. Instead, it seeks to understand *why* the system made the error seem like the right action to the human (C) Long meetings (D) Unnecessary documentation
Answer: B - Explanation: 'Human error is a symptom, not a cause.' If a human typing the wrong command brings down production, the root cause is a fragile system lacking safeguards, not a bad human.
Q72. What is 'OODA Loop' in the context of IT strategy? (A) A coding framework (B) Observe, Orient, Decide, Act. A cycle of continuous learning and adaptation, particularly useful in complex, rapidly changing competitive environments (C) A physical loop of network cable (D) A vendor selection matrix
Answer: B - Explanation: Originally developed for fighter pilots, the OODA loop is highly relevant to High-Velocity IT. The organization that iterates through the loop faster wins the market.
Q73. How does 'Design Thinking' approach problem solving? (A) By writing code immediately (B) By starting with deep empathy for the user, clearly defining their problem, ideating diverse solutions, prototyping, and testing iteratively (C) By copying competitors exactly (D) By asking the CEO what to build
Answer: B - Explanation: Design thinking ensures you build the 'right thing' before you worry about building the 'thing right'.
Q74. In managing Cloud Suppliers, what is 'Vendor Lock-in'? (A) Securing physical servers (B) The situation where a customer becomes so dependent on a specific cloud provider's proprietary tools or APIs that switching to another provider becomes exorbitantly costly or technically impossible (C) A long-term discount (D) A security measure
Answer: B - Explanation: Using basic AWS EC2 (VMs) is low lock-in. Rebuilding your entire architecture around AWS Lambda (serverless) creates high lock-in, which isn't always bad, but must be a strategic choice.
Q75. How does 'FinOps' help manage multi-cloud environments? (A) By writing better networking code (B) By bringing financial accountability to the variable spend model of cloud, ensuring engineering teams understand the cost implications of their architectural choices in real-time (C) By firing Expensive developers (D) By buying cheaper hardware
Answer: B - Explanation: In the old days, procurement bought servers. In the cloud, developers can spin up $100,000 of servers with an API call. FinOps teaches developers to engineer for cost optimization.
Q76. What is a 'Strangler Fig Pattern' used for in application modernization? (A) Killing off old employees (B) Incrementally migrating a legacy monolithic application by gradually replacing specific pieces of functionality with new microservices, until the old system can be safely strangled/decommissioned (C) A networking security topology (D) A physical wire arrangement
Answer: B - Explanation: Big-bang rewrites of legacy systems almost always fail. The strangler pattern allows for safe, incremental modernization.
Q77. What distinguishes 'Microservices' from a 'Monolith'? (A) Microservices are physically smaller computers (B) Microservices architect an application as a suite of small, independently deployable services that communicate over APIs, rather than a single, massive, tightly-coupled codebase (C) There is no difference (D) Monoliths are faster to deploy
Answer: B - Explanation: Microservices allow different teams to update, deploy, and scale different parts of an application independently, vastly increasing velocity, though adding networking complexity.
Q78. In a Microservice architecture, what happens if one non-critical service fails (e.g., the 'Recommendations' engine on a retail site)? (A) The entire website crashes (B) The architecture is designed for graceful degradation; the rest of the site (checkout, search) continues to function normally, avoiding a catastrophic outage (C) The database deletes itself (D) The user must reboot their computer
Answer: B - Explanation: This is a core resilience pattern. Loosely coupled services prevent localized failures from cascading.
Q79. What is 'Chaos Engineering' attempting to validate? (A) That employees can work under stress (B) That the system's automated resilience mechanisms (like auto-scaling, graceful degradation, and load balancing) actually work as designed when real failures occur (C) That the code compiles (D) That the data is secure
Answer: B - Explanation: You don't know if your backup system works until it's tested. Chaos engineering tests these mechanisms constantly by intentionally breaking things.
Q80. What is 'A/B Testing'? (A) Testing hardware vs software (B) An experiment where two or more variants of a page or feature are shown to users at random, and statistical analysis is used to determine which variation performs better for a given business outcome (C) Testing backup tapes (D) Testing network cables
Answer: B - Explanation: A/B testing removes guesswork. Instead of arguing over which button color is better, you test both and let the user data decide.
Q81. How does 'High-Velocity IT' view the concept of 'Risk'? (A) Risk must be eliminated (B) Managing risk is essential, but avoiding all risk guarantees failure in a fast-moving market. The goal is to make failure cheap, fast, and survivable (safe-to-fail experiments) (C) Risk is only for financial institutions (D) Risk should be ignored
Answer: B - Explanation: Speed inherently involves risk. High-velocity organizations build tools (like blue/green deployments and canary releases) that allow them to take calculated risks safely.
Q82. Which ITIL practice is arguably elevated to the highest strategic importance by the BRM role? (A) Incident Management (B) Strategy Management and Portfolio Management (C) Service Desk (D) Event Management
Answer: B - Explanation: The BRM's job is to ensure the IT Portfolio investments perfectly align with the broader enterprise Strategy.
Q83. What is an 'Information Radiator'? (A) A server that overheats (B) A highly visible display (like a large physical or digital Kanban board on a wall) that shows the team's current work, metrics, or build status at a glance without anyone needing to ask (C) A newsletter (D) A firewall
Answer: B - Explanation: Information radiators support the 'Collaborate and Promote Visibility' principle by making crucial data unignorable and effortlessly accessible.
Q84. What is the fundamental difference between 'Push' and 'Pull' work systems? (A) Pushing is faster (B) In a 'Push' system, management assigns work to teams regardless of capacity. In a 'Pull' system (like Lean/Kanban), teams pull new work only when they have the capacity to handle it, preventing overload and bottlenecks (C) There is no difference (D) Pulling requires more managers
Answer: B - Explanation: Pull systems prevent the massive buildup of Work in Progress (WIP), ensuring smoother flow and higher eventual throughput.
Q85. What does the term 'Shift-Right' mean in emerging DevOps practices? (A) Moving all work to a different time zone (B) Testing and monitoring in the live production environment using real user traffic, acknowledging that staging environments can never truly replicate the complexity of real production (C) Outsourcing (D) Delaying deployment
Answer: B - Explanation: While Shift-Left tests code before deployment, Shift-Right uses tools like Dark Launching and Observability to test code safely *after* it's live.
Q86. What is 'Gamification' in the context of organizational change? (A) Playing video games at work (B) Applying game-design elements and principles (like points, leaderboards, or specific challenges) in non-game contexts to encourage engagement, adoption of new tools, or learning (C) Banning board games (D) Rewriting software code
Answer: B - Explanation: Gamification can effectively overcome resistance to change by making the adoption of a new process more engaging or rewarding.
Q87. What does the 'Conway’s Law' state? (A) Software always has bugs (B) Organizations which design systems are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organizations (C) Moore's law is dead (D) Networks must be fast
Answer: B - Explanation: If you have four siloed teams (UI, Backend, Database, Security), they will inevitably build an architecture with exactly four distinct, clunky interfaces mirroring their teams.
Q88. How does 'Inverse Conway Maneuver' address Conway's Law? (A) By ignoring it (B) By deliberately restructuring the human organizational chart (e.g., creating cross-functional teams combining UI, Backend, DB, Security) to encourage the emergence of a better, modern software architecture like microservices (C) By firing the architects (D) By buying different software
Answer: B - Explanation: You change the people's communication structure first, knowing the software architecture will naturally follow.
Q89. What is 'Value Stream Management' (VSM - as a software tool category)? (A) Drawing on whiteboards (B) Software platforms that integrate with DevOps tools to provide visibility across the entire software delivery lifecycle, tracking metrics like Lead Time and identifying bottlenecks automatically (C) Accounting software (D) Intrusion detection
Answer: B - Explanation: Modern VSM tools aggregate data from Jira, GitHub, Jenkins, and ServiceNow to provide a single dashboard showing exactly how fast value is flowing.
Q90. What is a 'Hackathon' often used for in corporate cultures? (A) Stealing competitor data (B) A structured event where cross-functional teams collaborate intensively over a short period (e.g., 24-48 hours) to rapidly prototype innovative solutions to specific problems (C) Breaking into mainframes (D) Writing documentation
Answer: B - Explanation: Hackathons foster innovation, break down silos, and provide a safe space for high-velocity experimentation.
Q91. In a multi-supplier environment, what is 'Service Integration'? (A) Writing APIs (B) Exploring the coordination of multiple suppliers to ensure they work together seamlessly to deliver a single, end-to-end service to the business (C) Creating a single mega-vendor (D) Ignoring external vendors
Answer: B - Explanation: It's the core of the SIAM (Service Integration and Management) model, ensuring one vendor's SLA doesn't conflict with another's.
Q92. What is an 'Ethics' concern regarding Generative AI in IT Service Management? (A) The AI might demand a salary (B) The potential for AI to hallucinate inaccurate resolutions, exhibit inherent biases based on training data, or inadvertently expose sensitive company data (C) The AI is too slow (D) The AI refuses to work on weekends
Answer: B - Explanation: High-velocity adoption of AI must be tempered with ethical governance to prevent data leaks and ensure decisions affecting humans are transparent and fair.
Q93. What does 'Digital Native' mean referring to an organization? (A) An organization that creates hardware (B) An organization born in the digital era, inherently structured around modern technology, agile principles, and data-driven decision making, lacking legacy structural baggage (C) An organization that only uses social media (D) An organization that sells software exclusively
Answer: B - Explanation: A company like Netflix is digital native. A 100-year-old bank trying to adopt cloud is undergoing 'Digital Transformation'.
Q94. What is the fundamental goal of a 'Target Operating Model' (TOM)? (A) Designing the physical office (B) To define the desired future state of the organization's structure, processes, capabilities, and culture necessary to execute its strategic vision (C) To list software bugs (D) To track daily hours
Answer: B - Explanation: The TOM is the blueprint of what the organization wants to become during its transformation journey.
Q95. How does 'Customer Journey Mapping' differ from 'Value Stream Mapping'? (A) They are identical (B) Value Stream Mapping looks internally at the steps required to deliver an outcome. Customer Journey Mapping looks externally, documenting the Emotional and experiential steps a customer goes through when interacting with the service (C) Customer journey mapping is for hardware (D) Value stream mapping is imaginary
Answer: B - Explanation: The customer journey maps the touchpoints and frustrations (e.g., 'the user felt confused on the login screen'). The value stream maps the internal IT process.
Q96. What is 'Design for Operations'? (A) Building beautiful UI (B) The practice of ensuring that non-functional requirements (like maintainability, observability, and operability) are built into the software from day one, rather than treated as an afterthought (C) Ignoring developers (D) Buying expensive servers
Answer: B - Explanation: If you design a brilliant system that is impossible to monitor or update safely without downtime, it is a failed design.
Q97. What does the term 'Bimodal IT' refer to, and how is it currently viewed? (A) Using two monitors (B) An older concept of splitting IT into Mode 1 (slow, stable legacy) and Mode 2 (fast, agile digital). It is now largely criticized because it creates deeper silos and starves the 'legacy' systems of necessary modernization (C) Using Mac and PC (D) Using two cloud providers
Answer: B - Explanation: ITIL v5 and modern DevOps prefer applying agile principles holistically to all systems, rather than creating a two-class corporate structure.
Q98. In 'Capacity Management' for the Cloud, how does the approach shift? (A) It becomes solely about negotiating bulk discounts on physical servers (B) It shifts from long-term capital expenditure (CapEx) planning for hardware, to real-time operational expenditure (OpEx) automated scaling and FinOps constraints (C) It is no longer needed (D) It requires longer planning cycles
Answer: B - Explanation: You no longer predict capacity 3 years out. You write auto-scaling rules that add server instances automatically when CPU hits 80%, and tear them down when demand drops.
Q99. What is a 'Service Level Pattern' (often called an 'Anti-pattern') to avoid? (A) Measuring outcomes (B) Defining SLAs that are overly complex, technically focused, and entirely disconnected from the actual business value being delivered (C) Communicating with users (D) Tracking tickets
Answer: B - Explanation: Good SLAs/XLAs are simple, understandable by the business sponsor, and directly tied to the desired outcome.
Q100. Final Question: In a modern, complex multi-supplier environment, who ultimately retains the accountability for the overall service delivered to the customer? (A) The Cloud Provider (AWS/Azure) (B) The specific vendor who caused the outage (C) The enterprise Service Provider acting as the Service Broker integrating all the downstream suppliers (D) The end-user
Answer: C - Explanation: You can outsource the execution and the responsibility to a vendor, but you cannot outsource your ultimate accountability to your customer. If AWS goes down, your customer blames YOU, not AWS.
